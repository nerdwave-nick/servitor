Invoke an aspect of a rite, or proclaim an edict, verse by verse

Invocation is the servitor's foremost ritual. Name a rite and one of its
aspects, and the servitor brings the machine into that aspect by performing
the rite's liturgy — its sanctums, transcriptions, tethers, incantations,
litanies and vox-casts — one step after another, exactly in the order
written. Every rite of the Librarium stands beneath this ritual as a ritual
of its own, so the faithful write: servitor invoke <rite> <aspect>.

An edict knows no aspect and is proclaimed by its name alone: servitor
invoke <edict>. Any word written after its name is refused before a verse
is performed, for an edict bears no aspect. Every invocation of an edict
performs its whole liturgy anew; nothing is passed over because it was
performed before.

First comes the pre-flight, which touches nothing: every placeholder is
illuminated, every tome read afresh, every vessel, tether and tongue weighed,
and every inscription resolved. Should the pre-flight find any heresy, the
invocation is forbidden, each heresy is recited, and nothing on the machine
is disturbed.

Then the liturgy is performed. Should a step fall — a vessel that cannot be
written, an incantation that ends bearing a death-mark, a patience that runs
out — the steps already performed are reverted in reverse order, the fallen
step among them, and the lament tells which verse fell, its last words, and
how each reversion went. An interrupt from the terminal (Ctrl-C) halts the
invocation in the same manner: the labouring command and its kin are slain,
no further step begins, and every deed is undone; a second interrupt during
the reversions is not heeded, so that the reversions may finish.
SIGTERM halts it alike. A hangup does not: the departure of the one who
invoked it is no command to undo, and a reader gone from stdout or stderr
loses only the words, never a deed. Lesser machines that do not wait launch
the servitor detached, as its own passage teaches.

A rite of pattern Mark II keeps no liturgy, but subjects (see subjects). Its
invocation reads every subject first, as the augury reads it; the former
aspect is the one their omens agree upon. A subject already bearing the omen
of the aspect invoked is passed over; every other — one whose omen differs,
whose auspex is mute, or that has no auspex — is set, in written order: a
sanctum, transcription or tether written as its step of Mark I would be, a
machine-spirit by its enact words for the omen commanded. Each is proven at
once by a second reading, which must show that omen; a machine-spirit
without an auspex cannot be proven. A subject that shows another omen fells
the invocation, and every subject set is restored, as the passage on
reversion tells. When no subject differs, nothing is performed, and the
invocation is still a triumph: the data-slate is written, the chronicle
kept, and a "success" vox-cast still speaks. The report names each subject
set, "✔ subject smt · machine-spirit".

The observances of such a rite are spoken in place, among the subjects that
change, never deferred: before a changing subject, each of its introits not
yet spoken; after it, each recessional of which it is the last changing
subject to name it — once each, and only when its guards "to" and "from"
admit the invocation. They are spoken as incantations and litanies are, in
their tongue, within their patience. The pre-flight examines every
observance, spoken or not, before anything is touched. An observance that
falls fells the invocation as a subject does; the report names each one
spoken, "✔ observance veil · incantation", yet "subject x / n" counts the
subjects alone.

A rite or edict that holds exalted verses is performed alike, yet its
exalted verses never with its invoker's authority. Its pre-flight also draws
them for the aspect invoked into their unit and compares its sigil with the
sanction of their master: a plan never sanctioned, or whose sanction has
lapsed, is refused before any proof is asked, and the words name servitor
sanction <rite>. It judges the servitor installed at /usr/bin/servitor and
finds the intercessor; it refuses a rune given for an inscription spliced
into exalted words, and a word given at invocation that exalted speech could
not hear (more than 4 KiB, or bearing a NUL or a newline). After the
pre-flight and before the first verse one line tells, upon a terminal, that
the exalted verses await the master's proof, naming the intercessor and the
sigil (upon stderr under --binharic, withheld by --silence; without a
terminal the intercessor's own dialog speaks), and the intercessor asks the
proof once. The one exalted servitor it raises performs each exalted verse in
its turn, from the sanctioned copy alone, while the servitor performs the
common verses between them; should any verse fall, the exalted servitor
reverts its verses among the common reversions, from the fallen verse back to
the first, and no second proof is asked. A proof dismissed or refused is a
refusal before anything was touched. Every invocation asks the proof anew.

Each inscription the rite declares becomes a rune of its own, --<key> <value>,
which outranks the rite's decree for that aspect (or the edict's one decree);
an empty word clears it. A mandatory inscription that neither rune nor decree
supplies forbids the invocation. The rune --foresee (-f) reveals everything
the invocation would do and performs none of it; --silence (-s) withholds the
report, save the vox-casts the rite asks for. The rune --binharic speaks
upon stdout, for lesser machines, the very line the invocation added to the
chronicle — or, should the pre-flight forbid it, the heresies under
"forbidden" — and tells the report upon stderr instead; the desktop hears
as it always would. --binharic and --foresee are not spoken together.

Spoken at a terminal, the invocation is told in one report as it is
performed: a header naming the rite and its turning, the former aspect and
the new; a line for every verse once it is performed, the home written ~
and a long verse cut short with … to the width of the terminal; every
progress vox-cast in its place, with how many of the steps that act upon
the machine are done; and one closing line of triumph, drawn from the
rite's success vox-cast or, should it have none, from the same litany:

  ❯ servitor invoke mouse-autohide-toggle on
  +++ mouse-autohide-toggle · off → on +++
    ✔ verse 1 · sanctum ~/.config/niri/util.kdl
    ✔ verse 2 · incantation niri msg action do-screen-transition
  ✠ The Omnissiah is pleased: the rite now bears the aspect «on».

Exalted verses await the master's proof before the header, the proof asked
in the intercessor's own words:

  ❯ servitor invoke split-lock on
  ⋯ The exalted verses of split-lock await the master's proof: sudo asks it, under the sigil 4f0c2a9e1b7d.
  servitor: authenticate to perform split-lock (sigil 4f0c2a9e1b7d):
  +++ split-lock · off → on +++
    ✔ verse 1 · incantation echo off > /sys/devices/system/cpu/smt/control
    ✔ verse 3 · incantation sysctl -wq kernel.split_lock_mitigate=0
  ✠ The Omnissiah is pleased: the rite now bears the aspect «on».

An edict's header names the edict alone, with no turning of aspects, and its
closing line names the edict, never an aspect:

  ❯ servitor invoke screenshot
  +++ screenshot · edict +++
    ✔ verse 1 · incantation grim -g "$(slurp)" ~/pictures/full.png
  ✠ The edict screenshot is proclaimed, and the machine obeys.

The closing words are drawn at random. Roused without a terminal — from a
hotkey or a launcher — the servitor sends the vox-casts to the desktop
instead, and tells the same report once the rite has triumphed. Should a
step fall, the report ends with the last verse performed, and the lament
alone tells the fall.

After a performance the servitor writes the rite's data-slate and adds one
line to the chronicle, whatever the verdict. An invocation the pre-flight
forbade, or one merely foreseen, is neither chronicled nor recorded on the
data-slate, for nothing was performed.

The servitor's death-mark tells how the invocation ended, with or without
--binharic: 0 upon triumph, 1 when it was reverted, 2 when it was refused
before anything was touched (the pre-flight forbade it, the rite or aspect
is unknown, a word was written after an edict's name, a rune was
malformed, its exalted verses stand unsanctioned or lapsed, no intercessor
vouched for the master, the proof was dismissed), and 3 when it faltered.

The servitor itself performs nothing with the machine's own authority; the
exalted servitor, raised for one invocation, performs its exalted verses.
Roused as root
— through sudo, run0 or pkexec, or by root itself — it refuses every
invocation and every proclaiming with the death-mark 2 before anything is
touched; only --foresee is still heeded. Rouse it as yourself. Under sudo the
foresight reads the Librarium, the vessels and the data-slates of the one who
spoke sudo, as the passage on the Librarium tells.

A rite's scripture, kept in <Librarium>/rites/<name>.json, may read thus:

  {
    "pattern": "Mark I",
    "purpose": "Hide the mouse cursor after inactivity",
    "aspects": ["on", "off"],
    "inscriptions": {"reason": {"purpose": "why the rite was invoked"}},
    "liturgy": [
      {"sanctum": "~/.config/niri/util.kdl",
       "scripture": {"on": "cursor {\n    hide-after-inactive-ms 400\n}",
                     "off": ""}},
      {"incantation": "niri msg action do-screen-transition"},
      {"vox-cast": "success"}
    ]
  }

Kindred passages: rite, edict, liturgy, pre-flight, reversion, inscriptions,
verdicts, binharic, forbidden, death-marks, detached, dominion, exalted,
sanction, intercessor

Exempla:
  servitor invoke mouse-autohide-toggle on --reason "gaming remnant"
  servitor invoke mouse-autohide-toggle off --foresee
  servitor invoke theme "$(printf 'default\nfinii\nporpl\n' | fuzzel --dmenu)"
  servitor invoke screenshot --region full
  servitor invoke theme porpl --binharic | jq -r .verdict
