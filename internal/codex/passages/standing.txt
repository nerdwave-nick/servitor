How a rite stands: performed, dormant, corrupted, tainted and the rest

Every augury and every census judges how each rite stands, weighing the
omens of its subjects against its data-slate. Seven standings are known to
the codex:

  performed    the omens agree on one aspect, or on several among which the
               data-slate's aspect stands; or no subject yields an omen and
               the data-slate names the aspect last invoked
  dormant      neither omen nor data-slate names an aspect: the rite was
               never invoked, and nothing in the machine bears its mark
  corrupted    the omens agree on no aspect at all — one subject fits none
               of the aspects the others fit; or the last invocation
               faltered and could not be wholly reverted
  tainted      other hands altered the scripture within a sanctum: it no
               longer holds what its marker's aspect commands
  desecrated   the omens agree on an aspect the data-slate did not record,
               or on several of which none is the data-slate's — the
               machine was changed outside the servitor
  heretical    the rite's scripture is invalid, and nothing can be read
  proclaimed   for an edict alone: its last invocation ended in triumph or
               was reverted

A rite holds the gravest standing that applies, from heretical down through
corrupted, tainted and desecrated to performed or dormant.

The omens agree by intersection: a subject whose omen fits none of the
aspects the others fit corrupts the rite, though its omen be shared by two
aspects. Where the omens fit several aspects at once and no data-slate was
ever written, the rite lies dormant. A stale omen, taken from the
data-slate's record where no live one is heard, never desecrates a rite on
its own. The passage on the omens tells all.

An edict stands in no aspect and yields no omen, so it is judged by the
verdict of its last invocation alone: dormant until it is first proclaimed,
proclaimed after a last invocation that ended in triumph or was reverted,
corrupted after one that faltered — until it is proclaimed again — and
heretical when its scripture is. An edict is never performed, tainted or
desecrated, and no rite of aspects is ever proclaimed.

Desecration is no heresy. When a rite is invoked elsewhere — a bar that
flips do-not-disturb, a hand that re-points a tether — the servitor reports
the true aspect, marks the rite desecrated and names the aspect its
data-slate remembers as "former"; the next invocation through the servitor
cleanses it. The inquisition notes desecrated rites and tainted sanctums as
impurities; a heretical rite stands accused of heresy.

In binharic, an augury speaks "standing", "desecrated" (true or false),
"former" (only when desecrated), "fitting", "omens", and "taint" — the
sanctums other hands altered, each named by its subject; the augury of an
edict speaks its "standing" alone among these. The census bears "standing" and "desecrated"
for every rite, an edict's desecrated ever false.

How a rite stands towards the sanction of what it holds exalted — sanctioned,
lapsed or unsanctioned — is told apart, under "sanction"; the passage on the
sanction tells all.

Kindred passages: omens, data-slate, augury, census, heresy, edict, sanction
